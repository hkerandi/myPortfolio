03 — In progress, built in public

Time-series pipeline · Data quality · F1 telemetry

Data in Motion

A pipeline for 670K+ rows of real Formula 1 telemetry from the 2022 Italian Grand Prix, built to catch bad data early, then turn the clean data into something you can see and replay.

Role
Solo: design, build, document
Stack
Python, FastF1, Nim, PostgreSQL, TimescaleDB, Docker
Data problem
Gaps, duplicates, missing laps and non-race cars
Outcome so far
Working lap viewer that flags bad data; database load next
The real question

What does it take to make high-frequency data reliable enough to build on?

The problem
Public F1 telemetry arrives uneven, with gaps, duplicate timestamps and even vehicles that aren't race cars. Every downstream decision, from a pit call to a replay, inherits those errors. The job is to catch them before they spread.
The system
Python and FastF1 extract each session and normalise every time column to plain seconds, so nothing downstream has to parse durations. Nim does the heavy lifting (ingestion, validation, resampling) because it's compiled and fast. PostgreSQL with TimescaleDB, a time-series database, runs in Docker so anyone can reproduce the stack with one command; loading the cleaned data into it is the current step. Flags, a desktop lap viewer and a WebXR ride-along will all read from the same cleaned data.
The execution
The Nim lap viewer reads only the rows it needs, drops incomplete ones, sorts and de-duplicates timestamps, then interpolates between samples to replay any driver's fastest lap at true speed. Laps with gaps over two seconds are flagged on screen: bad data is shown, not hidden. When F1 locked its public timing archive in October 2026, I switched sources, documented the change, and kept the project moving.
The evidence
The repository, with a log of every data problem found so far: a source lockout, non-race vehicles (cars 241–243) in the position feed, 89 "missing" laps explained by retirements, uneven sampling, and no heading data, each with how it's handled.
The next step
Load and validate the data in TimescaleDB, add tests and CI, then automated data-quality checks and flags. After that: Ride as Verstappen, see the data, a WebXR lap that reveals the raw samples, the cleaning and the flags on track.

babe!

A private diary for the days you're fine, and the ones you're not. It began as a way to log your health and grew into a way to reach your people sooner.

Role
Solo: design, build, testing
Stack
React Native, Expo, SQLite
Data problem
Health data that must never leave the phone, and never be lost
Outcome so far
In beta with 14 testers, 8 on iOS and 6 on Android
The problem
You stay busy. You say you're fine. You go quiet. The people who love you can tell something's up; they just don't know how to reach in.
What it does today
A daily diary for body feelings, wins, a photo and the song you had on repeat. A cycle calendar with a history strip for every cycle. A searchable sick log that turns a month, six months or the year so far into one message for your doctor. Go-to's for the hard days (remedies, recipes, products, places and people), sealed days that stay yours, and a face or fingerprint lock.
The data design
Local-first: everything lives on the device in SQLite, with no server, no account and no central store to breach. A normalised schema with idempotent upserts and additive, re-runnable migrations, so updates never lose entries. Cycles are derived from the days logged, not stored, keeping one source of truth.
The trade-off
No server means nothing to breach, but also no sync and no cloud backup. Encrypted local backup and restore is next, so privacy never costs anyone their history.
Where it's going
A gentle nudge when you've gone quiet, a one-tap "I need company" text, and a short note for your people on how to show up. It never diagnoses or predicts anything.

React Native · Expo · JavaScript · SQLite · Local notifications · Biometric authentication

What the data showed me

Most data isn't wrong. It just hasn't been seen from a different angle yet. Here's what each dataset showed me once I looked again, and what I did about it.

  • PA Prisons

    Monthly files stopped matching each other

    What was really going on
    Schema drift between releases: renamed, missing and unexpected columns
    How I handled it
    Validation that checks each month on its own before it reaches production
  • PA Prisons

    Columns that shouldn't exist, values that wouldn't match

    What was really going on
    Phantom columns, type mismatches and stray whitespace
    How I handled it
    Caught and cleaned at ingestion, so the app never sees them
  • F1 telemetry

    971 laps where 1,060 were expected

    What was really going on
    The 89 "missing" laps belonged to drivers who retired
    How I handled it
    Logged with the cause, never filled in with made-up laps
  • F1 telemetry

    Cars 241, 242 and 243 in the position feed

    What was really going on
    Non-race vehicles sharing the feed
    How I handled it
    Excluded by a written rule, so the choice can be checked
  • F1 telemetry

    Gaps over two seconds, repeated timestamps

    What was really going on
    Uneven sampling and duplicate rows
    How I handled it
    Duplicates removed; gaps flagged on screen instead of smoothed over
  • F1 telemetry

    The source went dark

    What was really going on
    F1 locked its public timing archive in October 2026
    How I handled it
    Switched sources and documented why
  • Billboard Hot 100

    50+ years of records that didn't agree

    What was really going on
    Decades of inconsistent types, whitespace and edge cases
    How I handled it
    Normalised into one reproducible pipeline over 152K+ records
  • Orientation planning

    The same people, counted twice

    What was really going on
    90+ people spread across 6 spreadsheets
    How I handled it
    Python scripts to de-duplicate and reconcile; planning time down 40%
  • babe!

    Cycle history that could drift from what was logged

    What was really going on
    Storing the same fact twice
    How I handled it
    Derived from daily entries instead: one source of truth
